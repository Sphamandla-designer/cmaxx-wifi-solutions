Fast Internet Does Not Automatically Mean Good WiFi

A fast fibre, LTE or 5G connection can deliver excellent speed to the main router while rooms further away experience weak signal, buffering or frequent disconnections.

The internet connection brings connectivity into your property. Your WiFi network is responsible for distributing that connectivity to your phones, computers, televisions, cameras and smart devices.

CMAXX identifies where the problem is occurring and designs the internal network needed to make better use of the internet service you already have.

An internet speed test is not a coverage test

A speed test measures the connection available to the device being tested at that moment. It does not prove that every room has the same signal or performance. The line may be delivering its expected speed at the router while the signal weakens through walls or floors, a distant device connects at a lower wireless rate, interference reduces stability, an older phone or television cannot match a newer device, or a mesh node receives a weak connection from the main node.

CMAXX compares wired and wireless performance where appropriate to determine whether the limitation comes from the internet service, the router, the WiFi network, the cabling or the connecting device.

Professionally installed access points

Multiple access points can distribute WiFi more effectively than relying on one router to cover an entire large property. CMAXX considers placement, mounting position, available cabling, radio overlap, channel use and the devices that need to connect.

Mesh WiFi

Mesh can suit homes where a flexible or less invasive deployment is needed. Where possible, wired backhaul improves consistency by letting the units communicate over Ethernet rather than relying entirely on wireless links.

A mesh product is not automatically the correct answer for every property. Performance still depends on placement, building materials, interference and the connection between mesh points.

Wired access-point solutions

For larger homes, demanding applications or long-term reliability, strategically installed access points connected through network cabling may be preferable — giving more predictable backhaul, better placement, centralised PoE power, easier expansion, improved management and a cleaner long-term structure.

Where cabling is required, CMAXX coordinates the necessary structured network cabling and data points.

Router and gateway configuration

The main router or gateway remains an important part of the network. CMAXX can assess and configure internet handover, local IP addressing, DHCP, WiFi settings, guest access, device prioritisation where appropriate, network separation, security settings, and firmware and management configuration.

A router replacement is recommended only when the existing device is unsuitable, faulty, outdated or unable to support the required design.

Smart-home, CCTV and IoT connectivity

Modern homes may have televisions, doorbells, cameras, voice assistants, lighting controls, alarm equipment and other connected devices. CMAXX designs the WiFi and network foundation that supports these systems more reliably, and can separate selected devices from personal computers and private household data through network segmentation.

For broader automation, see smart home solutions. For surveillance, see CCTV and security solutions.

Home WiFi FAQs

Why is my fibre fast next to the router but slow in other rooms?

The fibre line may be performing correctly while the WiFi signal weakens because of distance, walls, floors, interference or poor access-point placement. CMAXX can compare the connection at the router with performance in the problem areas.

Will adding a WiFi extender solve my dead zones?

It may help in some circumstances, but an extender requires a usable signal and can reduce performance or create roaming problems when poorly positioned. An assessment determines whether an extender, mesh unit, access point or wired connection is more appropriate.

Is mesh WiFi better than access points?

Neither is universally better. Mesh can be practical where cabling is difficult. Wired access points often provide more predictable backhaul and scalability. The correct choice depends on the property and performance requirements.

Can you use my existing fibre router?

Yes, where it is suitable. CMAXX assesses the router's condition, configuration and capability before recommending replacement.

Can you cover a double-storey or large home?

Yes. The solution is designed around the layout, wall and floor construction, required rooms and available cabling. More than one access point may be needed.

Can WiFi be extended to a cottage or outbuilding?

Yes. Depending on distance and obstacles, the solution may use Ethernet, fibre, outdoor access points or a point-to-point wireless link — see outdoor WiFi solutions.

Do you have to run network cables?

Not in every project. Cabling is recommended where it provides a meaningful improvement in stability, backhaul or future scalability. CMAXX will discuss practical routing options before installation.

Can the whole house use one WiFi name?

Usually, yes. Compatible access points can broadcast a coordinated WiFi name. Roaming behaviour also depends on the connecting device, which ultimately decides when to move between access points.

Can you improve connectivity for smart-home devices and cameras?

Yes. CMAXX can improve coverage, review device placement and configure appropriate network separation where supported.

Are you an Internet Service Provider?

No. CMAXX designs, installs, configures, optimises and manages the network that operates on top of your fibre, LTE, 5G or other internet connection.

How much does a home WiFi solution cost?

Cost depends on the property, equipment, number of access points, cabling and installation requirements. CMAXX confirms the assessment scope and provides a quotation before work begins.

How long does installation take?

A simple adjustment or small installation may be completed during one visit. Larger homes, extensive cabling and outdoor or outbuilding connectivity may require additional time. The expected programme is confirmed in the quotation.

WiFi Is Part of Your Business Infrastructure

Business connectivity supports far more than web browsing. When the WiFi network is poorly designed, the internet line may appear to be the problem even when the actual limitation is coverage, congestion, cabling, configuration or unsuitable equipment.

Why one router is often not enough

A consumer router can work well in a small environment with modest requirements. Problems arise when one device is expected to provide coverage across multiple floors, connectivity through dense walls, capacity for many employees and devices, separate staff and guest access, central monitoring, network segmentation, reliable roaming, PoE-connected access points, controlled configuration and expansion into new areas.

Adding basic extenders may increase the number of visible WiFi signals without creating a properly coordinated business network.

Core Components of a Business WiFi Solution

Not every organisation requires every component. CMAXX selects the components needed for the approved design.

Access-point and capacity planning

Access points are positioned according to floor layout, wall construction, expected users, device density, application requirements, interference, mounting conditions, available backhaul and future expansion.

The quoted quantity follows assessment and design rather than an unsupported square-metre formula.

Structured network cabling

Wired backhaul is often an important foundation for commercial WiFi. CMAXX can install or coordinate Ethernet data points, Cat6 or other suitable cabling, patch panels, network cabinets, cable management, labelling, testing, inter-building connectivity and fibre links where appropriate.

Full detail lives on structured network cabling.

Power over Ethernet

PoE can provide power and data to compatible access points, cameras and VoIP devices through network cabling, reducing the need for separate plug points at every device and allowing centralised network power planning.

PoE budgets must be designed according to the connected equipment and switch capability.

Staff, guest and device separation

Different users and systems should not automatically share unrestricted access to the same network. Depending on requirements, CMAXX may configure staff WiFi, guest WiFi, CCTV networks, IoT networks, voice networks, management networks and departmental separation.

VLANs and firewall rules help control how these networks communicate. Segmentation reduces unnecessary exposure but does not replace complete cybersecurity policies, endpoint protection or staff security practices.

Branded portals, vouchers or guest analytics: see Public & Guest WiFi solutions.

Routers, gateways and firewall configuration

The router or gateway manages traffic between the internet and internal networks. The design may include internet handover, routing, DHCP, firewall rules, VLAN interfaces, VPN capability where required, traffic management, multiple WAN connections, failover, and monitoring and logging.

Failover can reduce the effect of a primary internet outage, but its performance depends on the secondary connection, configuration and service availability.

Managed switches

Managed switches support VLANs, PoE management, monitoring and network expansion. Selection should consider port quantity, PoE requirements, uplink capacity, network design, expansion, rack or cabinet space and management compatibility.

Centralised or cloud management

Compatible platforms allow CMAXX or your authorised team to manage access points, switches and gateways from a central interface — supporting configuration consistency, device visibility, firmware management, performance review, remote diagnosis, alerting and easier multi-site administration.

Redundancy and continuity planning

Businesses that cannot rely on one connectivity path can discuss secondary fibre, LTE or 5G, multi-WAN gateways, automatic failover, backup power for network equipment and redundant network components where justified.

Redundancy reduces risk but does not create an unconditional guarantee of uninterrupted connectivity.

Supporting cloud, voice and business applications

Microsoft 365, cloud platforms, VoIP and video conferencing depend on more than download speed. Consistent performance is also influenced by upload capacity, latency, jitter, packet loss, WiFi signal quality, network congestion, device capability, cabling, traffic prioritisation and the external service provider.

CMAXX designs the network foundation and can coordinate with your ISP or IT provider. For administration, email, cloud or backup support, see Cloud and Microsoft 365 services.

Internet Problems and WiFi Problems Are Not the Same

The internet connection is the service supplied to the property. WiFi is one method used to connect local devices to that service. A problem may exist in one or more of seven places — a proper assessment looks beyond a single speed-test result.

Layer 01

Internet service

The ISP line may be down, congested, incorrectly provisioned or experiencing external faults.

Layer 02

Router or gateway

Configuration problems, limited processing capability, damaged ports, firmware issues or unsuitable features.

Layer 03

Switching and cabling

A damaged cable, incorrect termination, failing power supply, limited switch port or PoE problem can affect connected access points.

Layer 04

WiFi coverage

Signal may be weak because of distance, walls, floors, placement or antenna orientation.

Layer 05

Wireless interference

Neighbouring networks and other radio-frequency activity can affect available airtime and stability.

Layer 06

Capacity

One access point may be serving more active devices or demanding applications than the environment supports effectively.

Layer 07

Client devices

A specific phone, laptop, television or camera may have outdated software, limited WiFi capability or a device-specific fault.

Common causes we find

Poor router or access-point placement

Equipment installed in cupboards, corners, low positions or behind dense obstructions may not cover the intended areas effectively.

Weak mesh links

A mesh node placed inside a dead zone cannot reliably distribute a connection it is not receiving properly.

Too few or too many access points

Too few may leave gaps. Too many poorly configured access points can increase interference and roaming complexity.

Incorrect channel use

Overlapping or congested channels can reduce available airtime and consistency.

Unsuitable equipment

The equipment may not support the required features, device load, backhaul or internet speed.

Faulty cabling or power

Access points can appear to be WiFi problems when the underlying issue is a cable, switch port, PoE supply or power adapter — see network cabling services.

Outdated or inconsistent configuration

Firmware, DHCP, addressing, security settings and duplicated routers can create instability.

ISP and client-device limitations

The internal network cannot provide more internet capacity than the active connection supplies, and a device may not support the same WiFi generation, channel width, band or antenna capability as another.

Outdoor Connectivity Options

01

Outdoor-rated access points

An outdoor access point distributes WiFi in an external area — pool and entertainment areas, patios, gardens, courtyards, yards, parking areas and camera locations near the main building.

The access point still needs correctly planned backhaul, power, placement and configuration.

02

Ethernet backhaul

An Ethernet cable can provide both data and PoE to a compatible outdoor access point.

Often appropriate where the cable distance is within design limits, a protected route is available, the device is reasonably close to the network cabinet, and a dependable wired connection is preferred.

03

Fibre backhaul

Fibre may be appropriate between buildings, over longer cable routes or where electrical isolation and high-capacity connectivity are important.

Fibre design must include the appropriate transceivers, terminations, protection and network equipment.

04

Point-to-point wireless links

A point-to-point link connects two fixed locations through a dedicated wireless bridge — a main house and cottage, two office buildings, a lodge and remote building, a workshop, a gatehouse, an outbuilding or a remote camera network.

The receiving building normally requires its own switch or access point to distribute connectivity locally.

A point-to-point link is not the same as general guest WiFi. It acts as backhaul between locations.

05

Point-to-multipoint networks

A point-to-multipoint design can connect one central location to several remote locations where the property layout and line-of-sight conditions make it suitable — several cottages, multiple farm buildings, a lodge with separate units or a commercial property with distributed structures.

The design requires careful planning of capacity, interference, line of sight and equipment positions.

06

Supporting CCTV, gates and IoT devices

Outdoor cameras and connected systems may require stable connectivity, sufficient upload or local network capacity, suitable power, appropriate network separation, weather-resistant equipment, reliable backhaul, remote-management access and consideration of the recording architecture.

A camera showing full WiFi signal does not automatically mean its video path is stable.

The complete route from the camera to the recorder, hub, cloud platform or viewing device must be considered — CMAXX coordinates the outdoor network with its CCTV and security solutions where required.

Internet Bandwidth and WiFi Capacity Are Different

An Event WiFi service has two major layers, and both must be designed together.

Layer one

Internet backhaul

The connection from the venue to the internet. Required capacity depends on concurrent use, upload and download requirements, streaming, payment and registration systems, cloud applications, media activity and acceptable performance expectations.

Layer two

Local WiFi infrastructure

This distributes connectivity throughout the venue. It depends on access-point quantity, placement, user density, radio design, interference, backhaul to each access point, client devices and configuration.

Installing more access points does not increase the venue's internet bandwidth. Increasing the internet connection does not solve weak access-point coverage.

What Is Structured Cabling?

Structured cabling is the organised system of Ethernet and fibre cabling, data outlets, patch panels, network cabinets and related components that connects the devices and systems throughout a property. Instead of running random cables from one device to another, a structured installation creates a planned, labelled and maintainable network foundation.

In a modern property, that infrastructure can connect computers, printers, WiFi access points, CCTV cameras, VoIP phones, intercoms, smart TVs, servers, network switches, smart-home controllers and many other IP-based devices.

Good structured cabling is not only about achieving speed today. It should also make future changes easier, support Power over Ethernet where required, simplify troubleshooting, reduce unnecessary cable clutter and give the network room to grow.

Why the Cabling Behind Your Network Matters

A fast fibre line and a high-performance router cannot compensate for poor physical infrastructure. Damaged cable, low-quality conductors, poor terminations, excessive cable lengths, incorrect connectors, badly organised cabinets and unsuitable cable routes can all create instability, reduced link speeds or intermittent faults.

CMAXX looks at the network as a complete system. We consider the internet connection, router or firewall, switches, cable routes, cable category, PoE requirements, access points, cameras, end devices and future expansion before recommending the right infrastructure.

The network is only as reliable as the physical path carrying the data. CMAXX builds that foundation properly.

Cat5, Cat5e, Cat6 and Cat6A: What Is the Difference?

Ethernet cables may look similar from the outside, but their performance categories are not the same. Choosing the right category depends on the required speed, cable length, equipment, PoE needs, environment and how long the infrastructure is expected to remain in service.

Cable category Typical capability CMAXX guidance Cat5 Legacy Category cabling. Commonly associated with older 10/100 Mbps networks. Obsolete for new professional installations. Existing Cat5 should be assessed before relying on it for a modern network. Cat5e Supports Gigabit Ethernet over a standard channel up to 100 m when correctly installed. Rated to 100 MHz. Still common in existing properties and may remain suitable for many Gigabit links, but it is not our first choice for most new long-life installations. Cat6 Supports 1 Gbps over a standard 100 m channel. Rated to 250 MHz. 10 Gigabit performance is distance- and environment-dependent and is typically limited to shorter runs. Strong default choice for many homes, SMEs, WiFi access points, CCTV and general network installations. Cat6A Designed for 10 Gigabit Ethernet over a full 100 m channel. Rated to 500 MHz. Recommended for higher-performance, enterprise, future-focused and 10GbE-ready installations where the budget and environment justify it.

For 10 Gigabit Ethernet, Cat6A is the predictable 100-metre solution. Cat6 may support 10GbE on shorter channels, but the achievable distance depends on factors such as alien crosstalk and the quality of the installed system. That is why CMAXX selects cable based on the actual application rather than simply choosing the highest category number on the box.

A cable labelled "Cat6" is not automatically a good Cat6 installation

Conductor material, connector quality, cable length, routing, bend radius, terminations and testing all matter.

CCA Cable vs 100% Copper Ethernet Cable

One of the most important differences customers rarely see is the material inside the cable. Some low-cost network cable is made from Copper-Clad Aluminium (CCA): an aluminium conductor with a thin copper coating. It can look similar to copper cable from the outside, but it is not equivalent to standards-compliant copper Category cabling.

CCA has higher electrical resistance than copper, which becomes especially important when the same cable is carrying power for PoE devices. It can also be less mechanically robust at terminations and may create reliability problems that are difficult to diagnose after the cable has already been installed inside ceilings, walls or conduit.

Solid copper vs stranded copper

These describe how a copper conductor is constructed — different from CCA, which is the conductor material itself.

Solid copper
Permanent horizontal cabling inside walls, ceilings, ducts and structured routes — a robust permanent link and lower conductor resistance for the same gauge.
Stranded copper
Flexible patch leads between a wall outlet or patch panel and network equipment — better suited to short cords that are moved and handled.
CCA
Aluminium-based rather than an all-copper conductor. Not CMAXX's recommended substitute for professional permanent Category cabling, and not to be confused with stranded copper.

UTP vs shielded cabling

Most standard office and residential networks use unshielded twisted-pair cabling, often written as U/UTP. In environments with higher levels of electrical noise or specific technical requirements, shielded designs such as F/UTP may be more appropriate.

Shielding is not a simple "better cable" upgrade. A shielded cabling system must be selected and installed correctly, including compatible components and the required bonding or grounding approach.

Use the cable construction that suits the environment. Do not pay for shielding that is not required, and do not install shielded cable incorrectly.

Indoor vs outdoor cabling

Indoor Ethernet cable is not automatically suitable for exposed outdoor use. Sunlight, temperature changes, moisture and physical exposure can degrade unsuitable cable jackets over time.

For outdoor, rooftop, perimeter or between-building routes, CMAXX selects UV-resistant outdoor-rated cable, protective conduit, suitable glands and weather-aware routing. Where distances or conditions make copper impractical, fibre may be the better backbone.

How far can Ethernet cabling run?

For standard twisted-pair Ethernet structured cabling, the normal design limit is a 100-metre channel. In a conventional layout, the permanent installed link is designed around a maximum of 90 metres, leaving the remaining allowance for patch cords and connection hardware.

If a required run is too long, the answer is not to keep extending copper cable and hope for the best. CMAXX can redesign the link using an intermediate network location or fibre backbone.

Longer is not better. Cable length, insertion loss, PoE voltage drop and the end-to-end application all need to be considered together.

PoE, Data Points, Cabinets and Fibre Backbones

The parts of the infrastructure that decide whether a network is easy to live with — or expensive to troubleshoot.

PoE-ready cabling for WiFi, CCTV, VoIP and intercoms

Power over Ethernet allows compatible devices to receive network data and electrical power through the Ethernet connection, reducing the need for a separate power point at every device location.

Common PoE devices include ceiling-mounted WiFi access points, IP CCTV cameras, VoIP phones, video intercoms, access-control devices and selected smart-building equipment.

A PoE-ready design is more than connecting a PoE switch. CMAXX considers cable quality, conductor size, cable length, power requirements, switch capacity, heat in large cable bundles and the device's PoE standard before finalising the infrastructure.

Data points, RJ45 outlets, patch panels and termination

A structured network should be easy to identify and maintain. Instead of leaving permanent cables loose in a cabinet or hanging from a wall, CMAXX terminates fixed cabling into labelled data outlets, keystone modules and patch panels where appropriate.

Typical data-point locations include workstations, home offices, TVs, printers, WiFi access points, CCTV camera positions, intercoms and server rooms.

Correct termination helps maintain cable performance, simplifies future moves and changes, and gives a technician a clear path from a room outlet back to the network cabinet.

Network cabinets & server rack installations

The network cabinet is the central point where structured cabling and active network equipment come together. A poorly organised cabinet can turn a simple fault into hours of unnecessary troubleshooting.

Depending on the project, a cabinet may include a router or firewall, network switches, PoE switches, patch panels, cable managers, power distribution, UPS equipment, fibre termination and spare space for future expansion.

Inherited a messy cabinet? Ask CMAXX for a network rack clean-up and documentation assessment.

Fibre cabling for backbones and longer distances

Copper Ethernet is excellent for normal access runs, but it is not the answer to every network link. Fibre is often the better choice for longer distances, high-capacity backbones or connections between buildings.

Single-mode fibre is commonly selected for longer-distance and scalable backbone links, while multimode fibre can be appropriate for shorter high-speed links depending on the transceivers, distance and system design.

Typical use cases include office backbones, warehouses, farms, campuses, estates and outbuildings.

Upgrading old or problematic cabling

Not every existing cable needs to be replaced simply because a newer category exists. The correct approach is to assess what is installed, what performance is required and whether the current infrastructure is actually creating a bottleneck.

CMAXX can inspect inherited cabling, trace unknown runs, identify legacy or damaged cable, check suspicious terminations, review link speeds and recommend targeted upgrades — particularly useful after a change of owner, a renovation or years of undocumented additions.

Where Cat5e remains healthy and meets the application, it may continue to serve the network.

Testing, labelling & documentation

A cable is not finished when it has simply been pulled through the ceiling. Each completed run should be terminated correctly, identified and checked before handover.

Depending on scope, CMAXX performs wire-map and continuity verification, checks terminations and link status, validates connectivity, labels data points and patch-panel positions, and documents the installed routes or port schedule.

Where a project requires formal standards-based certification, the testing method and reporting requirement should be specified separately before work begins.

Structured Cabling Questions Answered

The technical questions customers ask before booking a site survey.

What is structured cabling?

Structured cabling is an organised system of Ethernet or fibre cables, data outlets, patch panels, network cabinets and related components that connects devices throughout a building. It provides a cleaner, easier-to-maintain foundation than ad-hoc cables running directly between individual devices.

What is the difference between Cat5, Cat5e, Cat6 and Cat6A?

Cat5 is legacy cabling and is not recommended for new installations. Cat5e commonly supports Gigabit Ethernet up to a standard 100-metre channel. Cat6 provides improved performance and supports 1 Gigabit over 100 metres, while 10 Gigabit use is generally limited to shorter channels. Cat6A is designed for 10 Gigabit Ethernet over the full 100-metre channel.

Should I install Cat6 or Cat6A?

For many homes, SMEs, CCTV and WiFi access-point installations, Cat6 is an excellent choice. Cat6A is worth considering when you need 10GbE over longer runs, higher-performance enterprise infrastructure, additional future headroom or a long-life installation where the extra cable size and cost are justified. CMAXX recommends the category after assessing the application.

Does CMAXX recommend Cat5e for new installations?

Cat5e can still provide reliable Gigabit connectivity when it is correctly installed and remains common in existing buildings. For many new permanent installations, however, CMAXX will normally consider Cat6 or Cat6A first because the infrastructure is expected to remain in place for years.

What is CCA Ethernet cable, and does CMAXX use it?

CCA stands for Copper-Clad Aluminium: an aluminium core with a copper coating. It is cheaper than all-copper Category cable but has higher resistance and should not be treated as equivalent to standards-compliant copper structured cabling, particularly for long-life or PoE-heavy installations. For permanent professional structured cabling, CMAXX prioritises quality copper cabling appropriate to the required Category and application.

What is the difference between solid copper and stranded copper cable?

Solid-conductor copper cable is commonly used for fixed permanent building runs because it is suited to structured horizontal cabling. Stranded copper is more flexible and is commonly used for patch leads that connect equipment to wall outlets or patch panels. Both can be copper; CCA is a different conductor material altogether.

How long can an Ethernet cable run?

A standard twisted-pair Ethernet channel is normally designed around a maximum of 100 metres. A conventional permanent installed link is typically limited to 90 metres, leaving the remaining channel allowance for patch cords and connection hardware. For longer links, fibre or a different network design may be required.

Can Ethernet cabling power WiFi access points and CCTV cameras?

Yes. Compatible Power over Ethernet equipment can carry network data and electrical power over the Ethernet connection. PoE is commonly used for WiFi access points, IP cameras, VoIP phones and intercoms. The cable, switch, distance and device power requirement all need to be designed correctly.

Can you install Ethernet cable next to electrical power cables?

Data cabling should be routed with appropriate separation from mains electrical cabling and in accordance with applicable installation rules and the equipment and cabling specifications. CMAXX plans cable routes to reduce electrical interference and avoid unsafe or non-compliant shared pathways.

What is the difference between UTP and shielded cable?

Unshielded twisted-pair cable is suitable for many normal residential and office environments. Shielded cabling can be useful in electrically noisy environments or where a particular design requires it, but the shielding system must be implemented correctly. CMAXX selects the appropriate construction after assessing the site.

Do I need outdoor-rated Ethernet cable outside?

Only equipment that still has power can keep operating. A suitable UPS or backup-power design can support the recorder, PoE switch, router and selected cameras for a limited period. Where Homes Are Actually Vulnerable

Most residential incidents happen at a small number of predictable points. A useful home system covers those points properly rather than spreading thin cameras evenly around the building. The approach

The pavement, street-facing wall, gate motor and the last few metres before someone reaches your boundary. Wide-angle context: how many people, which direction, what vehicle.

Usually the hardest scene at night — headlights, street lights and deep shadow in the same frame.

The boundary

Perimeter walls, side alleys, the servant's quarters passage, the fence line behind the garage and any low or unlit stretch that is easy to climb.

Often the position where cabling is hardest and a solar or battery camera earns its place.

The entry points

Front door, back door, sliding doors, the yard door and the garage. Faces and detail matter more than width here, so the camera sits closer and lower.

Entrance hall, passage and open-plan areas, where the brief is usually evidence after the fact rather than constant observation.

Parked cars, the turning circle and the space between the gate and the garage — commonly where a vehicle is targeted.

Number-plate legibility needs a dedicated, tightly framed camera, not a wide overview.

Outbuildings and the yard

Cottage, flatlet, storeroom, tool shed, borehole, pump, generator, inverter and solar equipment.

These positions are often out of reach of the house network and drive the wireless or solar decision.

Outages, Installation and the Handover

The two questions homeowners ask most are what happens when the power or the internet goes, and what actually happens on installation day. Both deserve a straight answer.

When the internet goes down

A local NVR, smart hub or microSD camera can carry on recording without an internet connection, provided the camera, recorder, local network and power are still working. What stops is remote viewing on your phone, cloud recording and push notifications — those return when the line does.

When the power goes off

Only equipment that still has power keeps working. A suitably sized UPS or inverter can hold up the recorder, PoE switch and router for a period, which keeps the wired cameras recording and remote viewing alive. Battery and solar cameras run on their own power, but they still need the network they connect to.

Load-shedding, realistically

We size backup power for the CCTV equipment specifically and tell you how long it should hold rather than quoting a general figure. If the router is on backup but the fibre provider's equipment is not, remote viewing still stops — that is worth knowing in advance.

Practical information for planning. This is not legal advice.

Wireless and Wire-Free Are Not the Same Thing

This is the single most common misunderstanding in smart-camera buying, and it produces most of the disappointment. "Wireless" usually describes how the video gets to your network. It says nothing about how the camera gets power.

Powered Wi-Fi camera

Video travels over Wi-Fi; the camera runs on a mains or DC power cable. This is the most common "wireless" camera, and it is not wire-free.

Best where power is nearby but running data cable is impractical. Continuous recording is possible on selected models.

Battery wire-free camera

A rechargeable battery for power and Wi-Fi or a compatible hub for data. Genuinely no cables, which is why it is the answer at awkward positions.

Almost always event-based rather than continuous. Battery life moves with activity, signal, temperature and settings.

Solar-assisted camera

A battery camera with a panel that tops it up during daylight, reducing or removing the need to bring it down for charging.

Needs real sun on the panel. Covered in depth on Solar CCTV Camera Installation.

Where the confusion costs money

A customer buys four "wireless" cameras for a boundary wall with no power on it, then discovers each one needs a plug. Or they buy battery cameras for a busy driveway, and the batteries need charging every few weeks because the camera is triggered forty times a day. Both are avoidable by checking power and expected activity at each position first — which is what an assessment is for.

The Signal Survey Comes Before the Camera

Every wireless camera failure we are called out to is one of three things: not enough signal at the position, too many cameras sharing one access point, or an upstream line that cannot carry what the cameras want to send. All three are measurable in advance.

A phone showing three bars beside the camera is not a signal survey. Phones report optimistically, they hold a connection long after it has become unusable for video, and they tell you nothing about how the link behaves when the camera actually starts uploading.

A camera that drops for ninety seconds at the wrong moment has failed at the only job it had. So we measure at the mounting height and position, not standing on the lawn below it.

Cameras are uploaders, not browsers

Most Wi-Fi devices in a home mainly download. A camera does the opposite — it pushes a continuous or event-triggered stream upward, all day, from a fixed position that is often the furthest point from the router and behind a brick wall. Several cameras on the same access point compound it.

Where the wireless network cannot support that, adding cameras will not fix it. The network gets fixed first — see professional WiFi and network assessment.

What we check at each position

  • Signal strength measured at the actual mounting point and height
  • Which band the camera will connect on, and whether that band reaches the position at all
  • Interference from neighbouring networks, and channel congestion on the band in use
  • Wall and roof construction between the position and the nearest access point
  • Total camera count on each access point, and the upload capacity they will share together
  • Whether the router or mesh node covering that area has a wired backhaul or is itself on a weak link
  • The upstream line speed, since remote viewing and cloud recording depend on upload, not download
  • Where an extra access point, an outdoor unit or a point-to-point link would solve it properly

Where a Smart Camera Puts Its Footage

Smart cameras have more storage options than professional systems, and the choice has real consequences. A camera recording only to its own memory card is a camera that loses its evidence if it is taken.

microSD in the camera

The default on most smart cameras. Cheap, simple, and completely local — but the storage sits inside the camera, so if the camera goes, the footage goes with it.

Use a card rated for continuous surveillance recording. Ordinary phone cards fail early and silently.

Hub or base station

Selected ecosystems record to a central hub, keeping footage off the camera itself and simplifying multi-camera management.

Check which cameras in the range are hub-compatible before you buy a mixed set.

Supported cloud plans

Some brands offer cloud or cloud-assisted recording on an optional subscription, which gives an off-site copy.

Retention, features, cost and even availability differ by brand, plan and region — and it consumes upload bandwidth.

A recorder, where supported

Selected Wi-Fi cameras can also record to a compatible NVR or network storage device, mixing smart cameras with a more conventional recording core.

Compatibility must be confirmed for the specific models — it is not a general capability.

Detection, alerts and tuning

Where supported by the selected model and firmware.

  • Person, vehicle, pet and package detection to cut alerts that do not matter

No detection system eliminates every false alert. Rain, insects at night, moving shadows, headlights and washing on a line will still occasionally trigger a camera. Good tuning reduces that to a level where the alerts still get read.

Account Security, Sharing and Knowing the Limits

Two things decide whether a smart-camera system holds up over years rather than months: how well the account is secured, and whether the system was the right choice for the site in the first place.

The account is the system

With a smart-camera ecosystem, whoever controls the account controls every camera — live view, playback, settings and deletion. That account deserves more care than the cameras themselves.

  • A unique, strong password used nowhere else, never the one from the box
  • Multi-factor authentication enabled wherever the platform supports it
  • Named shared users with appropriate permissions instead of one password passed around
  • Access removed promptly when someone moves out, leaves or no longer needs it
  • Camera and app firmware kept current, applied after compatibility and risk checks
  • Router admin credentials changed too — the cameras sit behind it
  • Recovery details kept somewhere the owner can actually reach in an emergency

How long does a battery camera actually last between charges?

Manufacturers quote figures from ideal conditions. In practice it depends on how many times a day the camera is triggered, how long each recording runs, how often you open live view, the wireless signal quality, the temperature and the settings. A quiet side passage might go months; a camera facing a busy pavement might need charging every few weeks. We estimate from the actual position and tune detection to extend it.

Do wireless cameras need internet, or just Wi-Fi?

They need a local wireless connection to your router or a compatible hub to reach storage. Internet is mainly needed for remote viewing, cloud features and push notifications. Selected cameras keep recording locally to a memory card or hub during an internet outage, provided the camera, local network and power are still working.

Can I mix brands?

You can install cameras from different brands on the same property, but they will not share one app, one hub or one timeline — you end up managing several systems. Where a property needs more than a few cameras, staying inside one ecosystem, or moving to a professional recorder-based system, is usually the better outcome.

My camera keeps going offline. Is it faulty?

Usually not. Repeated dropouts are most often signal quality at the mounting position, an overloaded access point, a router that reboots on a schedule, or a power supply problem on a mains-powered camera. We diagnose the cause rather than replacing the camera and hoping — see CCTV Maintenance, Repairs & Upgrades.

Do I have to pay a monthly subscription?

Not necessarily. Many smart cameras record locally to a memory card or hub with no subscription. Cloud recording, extended retention and some advanced detection features are subscription-based on certain brands. We tell you before installation which features on your chosen system are free and which are not.

Can these cameras record all the time, or only on motion?

Selected mains-powered Wi-Fi cameras support continuous recording to a card, hub or compatible recorder. Battery cameras are designed around event-based recording — continuous recording would drain them in hours. If you need a full timeline rather than clips, that points to a mains-powered or wired camera at that position.

Is a smart camera secure? I have read about cameras being accessed.

The risk is almost always the account rather than the camera: reused passwords, no multi-factor authentication, default credentials left in place or a password shared widely. We set a unique strong password, enable multi-factor authentication where the platform supports it, use named shared users rather than one shared login, and keep firmware current. No connected device can be declared risk-free, but these steps address the way these accounts are actually compromised.

Can I add a wireless camera to my existing wired CCTV system?

Sometimes. Selected Wi-Fi cameras can be added to a compatible NVR, and some professional platforms accept a mix. Compatibility has to be confirmed for the specific recorder and camera models. Otherwise the wireless cameras run as a separate system with their own app, which is workable but worth knowing in advance.

Will a mesh Wi-Fi system be enough for my cameras?

It can be, if the mesh node covering the camera position has a strong connection back to the main node — ideally a wired one. A mesh node that is itself on a weak wireless link will not give a camera behind it a reliable connection, no matter how many bars the app shows. That is exactly what the signal survey establishes.

Yes, subject to the models being suitable for the positions you have in mind. We will survey first and tell you honestly if a camera you already own is going to struggle where you want it — before it is on the wall rather than after.

How a Solar Camera Actually Works

Understanding the chain explains every limitation these cameras have — and why an honest assessment matters more here than on any other camera type.

01 · The panel

A small photovoltaic panel converts daylight into charge. It does not power the camera directly — it tops up the battery, and it only does so when it is receiving actual sun.

Panel size, angle and orientation matter more than most buyers expect.

02 · The battery

A rechargeable battery powers the camera continuously, day and night. The panel's job is to put back more than the camera takes out over each 24-hour cycle.

When consumption exceeds charge for several days, the camera eventually shuts down.

03 · The camera

Normally sleeping, waking on motion detection to record a clip and send an alert. This is why solar cameras are event-based rather than continuously recording.

Every wake, recording and live-view session is a withdrawal from the battery.

04 · The connection

Either the property Wi-Fi, a compatible hub, or a SIM on a mobile network. Weak signal is expensive: the camera works harder and longer to send the same clip.

Connectivity is usually what decides between a solar Wi-Fi and a solar 4G camera.

The energy budget is the whole story

A solar camera is a small energy budget with a lens on it. If the panel puts back more charge each day than the camera spends on detections, recordings, live views and transmission, the system runs indefinitely. If it does not, the battery drains over days or weeks and the camera goes dark — usually in mid-winter, and usually at the least convenient time. Every decision on this page comes back to that balance.

The Site Factors That Decide Whether It Works

Two identical cameras on two farm gates ten kilometres apart can behave completely differently. Almost all of the difference is in these three areas, and all of them are assessable before anything is bought.

Sunlight, angle and shading

Assessed at the position, not estimated from the driveway.

  • In South Africa the panel faces north to catch the most sun through the day
  • Tilt is set for the site rather than left flat — a flat panel collects dust and sheds less rain
  • Shade from a tree, wall, gutter, water tank or pole is the most common cause of underperformance, and a branch that is small today will not be in two years
  • Winter is the design case: shorter days and a lower sun angle mean the panel that just coped in December may not in June
  • Dust and rain marks reduce output gradually, so the panel needs occasional cleaning
  • Hail, wind loading and the mounting bracket's own strength all matter on an exposed pole

Activity, settings and battery life

What the camera spends its charge on.

  • How often the camera is triggered — a gate on a busy road and a quiet paddock are entirely different loads
  • Clip length and resolution: longer, sharper recordings cost more energy to capture and to send
  • How often live view is opened, which is one of the heaviest single drains
  • Signal quality, because a weak link means transmitting longer for the same clip
  • Spotlight, siren and two-way audio use, where the model has them
  • Cold nights, which temporarily reduce usable battery capacity
  • Detection tuning — motion zones and person detection cut pointless wake-ups more than any other setting

Mounting, weather and access

A remote camera is only as good as its bracket.

  • High enough and secure enough that the camera and panel are not simply removed
  • Reachable for maintenance — panel cleaning, a firmware update or a battery swap should not need scaffolding
  • Pole, wall or post assessed for strength and movement; a camera that sways is a camera that false-triggers
  • Cable between panel and camera routed and protected against sun, rodents and livestock
  • Outdoor-rated housing appropriate to the exposure, with connections weatherproofed properly
  • Consideration of what happens if the camera is tampered with, since nobody is nearby to notice

Recording and retrieval at a remote point

Most solar cameras record clips to a memory card in the camera, with supported cloud recording as an option on some models. That matters because retrieving footage from a card means going to the camera — which is exactly the thing that is hard to reach. Where footage may be needed quickly, a cloud-capable model or a link back to a recorder at the main property is worth the extra cost.

Alerts are the point

At a remote gate or paddock, the alert usually matters more than the recording. Tuning detection so that a real event reaches your phone — and a cow, a branch or a passing bakkie does not — is most of the value in the installation. It also happens to be what makes the battery last.

When Solar Is the Wrong Answer

We install solar cameras regularly, and we also talk clients out of them regularly. A solar camera in the wrong position is an expensive way to be disappointed twice — once when it stops working, and again when it has to be replaced with the thing that should have gone in.

A cable could reach without much disruption

If a conduit already runs to the gate, or a trench can follow an existing service route, wired IP/PoE is more reliable, records continuously and never needs a battery thought about again. See structured cabling and PoE network infrastructure.

The position is genuinely busy

A camera triggered constantly will exhaust a solar budget. Busy entrances and thoroughfares want mains power and continuous recording, even if getting power there costs more.

You need a continuous timeline

Solar cameras record events, not a full day. Where you may need to scrub back through an unbroken timeline, that is a wired recorder's job.

The panel cannot get real sun

Under a dense tree, on a south-facing wall or inside a covered structure, a solar camera will run down. Better to bring power to it, or move the mounting point.

Several cameras in one remote area

Once a remote area needs three or four cameras, a point-to-point wireless link plus a small local power supply is usually cheaper, more reliable and easier to maintain than four independent solar units.

There is no mobile coverage at all

A 4G camera needs a usable signal at the mounting height. Where there is none, the answer is a wireless link back to the property rather than a SIM that will never connect.

Solar and Remote-Property CCTV Questions

Questions specific to solar, 4G and remote-site installations. Broader CCTV questions are answered on the CCTV & Security Solutions hub.

Do solar cameras work at night?

Yes. The camera runs from its battery day and night; the panel simply recharges that battery during daylight. Night vision works normally. What matters is whether each day puts back more charge than the previous 24 hours consumed.

What happens during a week of overcast weather?

The camera keeps running on stored battery charge. How long it lasts depends on the battery capacity, how busy the position is and how the camera is configured. A correctly sized system installed at a quiet position will usually ride out a poor week; a marginal installation at a busy one may not. This is exactly why we assess before recommending rather than after.

Does a solar camera need any maintenance?

Some. The panel needs occasional cleaning — dust and rain marks reduce output gradually enough that nobody notices until the camera starts dropping out. Batteries degrade over years and eventually need replacing, and vegetation grows into what was a clear line of sun. We factor a serviceable mounting position into the design for exactly this reason.

How much mobile data does a 4G camera use?

It varies widely with clip length, resolution, how often the camera is triggered and how often you open live view — live viewing is by far the heaviest use. We set expectations against the specific model and its settings, and recommend a plan with headroom. A bundle that runs out mid-month takes the camera offline just as effectively as a flat battery.

Can a solar camera record all the time?

In practice, no. Continuous recording would exhaust the battery in hours and the panel could not keep up. Solar cameras are designed around event-based recording. If you need an unbroken timeline at that position, it needs mains power and a recorder.

Will it work at a farm gate five kilometres from the house?

Often, yes, using a solar 4G camera if there is mobile coverage, or a solar Wi-Fi camera behind a point-to-point wireless link if there is line of sight back to the property. Which of the two is better depends on the terrain, the signal and whether you would rather pay for data or for infrastructure. We assess both.

What stops someone stealing the camera and the panel?

Mounting height, position and bracket choice, plus security fasteners where appropriate. It cannot be made impossible. Where theft risk is high, positioning the camera so that it also covers its own approach, and choosing a model that uploads clips off-site rather than only recording to an internal card, means an attempt still produces evidence.

Can I add a solar camera to my existing system?

Sometimes. Where the solar camera is from the same ecosystem as your existing smart cameras, it usually joins the same app and hub. Adding one to a professional NVR-based system depends on the specific camera and recorder supporting each other, which we confirm before recommending it.

Do you install solar cameras at construction sites?

Yes. Temporary sites are a good fit for solar 4G, since they need no permanent infrastructure and can move with the work. Mounting security, expected activity and how footage will be retrieved when the camera moves are the practical questions to settle first.

Is a solar CCTV camera the same as a solar-powered property?

No, and it is worth separating the two. A solar camera is a self-contained unit with its own small panel and battery, sized only to run that camera. It is not connected to a property solar installation and does not power anything else.

The System Audit and Health Check

Whether you have inherited a system, taken over a building, or simply have not looked at yours in three years, the starting point is the same: establish what is actually there and what is actually working.

A repair visit fixes what is broken today. A health check tells you what is about to break — and on a CCTV system, the failures that matter are almost always the silent ones.

A recorder can display six healthy live images while writing none of them to disk. Nobody notices until footage is needed, and by then the window has closed. Checking that recording is actually happening, and that the retention you believe you have is the retention you have, is the single most valuable thing a maintenance visit does.

The test that matters most

Pick a camera. Ask the system to play back that camera at a specific time three weeks ago. If it cannot, you do not have the system you think you have — and that is worth knowing now rather than the morning after something happens.

What Actually Fails, and What We Do About It

CCTV equipment lives outdoors, runs continuously and is rarely looked at. Failures cluster in predictable places — and knowing which is which saves you from replacing a perfectly good camera because of a two-metre length of damaged cable.

Cameras, lenses and housings

Failed image sensors, dead infrared illuminators, water ingress, hazed domes, seized brackets and cameras knocked out of alignment. Many are repairable; some are cheaper to replace.

We reseal and realign where it makes sense, and say when a camera is simply finished.

Cabling and connectors

Corroded outdoor joints, cable damaged by rodents, UV-perished sheathing, poorly terminated plugs and runs that were always marginal and have now failed.

Cable faults are the most common single cause, and the one most often misdiagnosed as a dead camera.

PoE switches and power

Failed switch ports, exceeded power budgets, dying plug-top power supplies, and cameras that brown out when a new one was added to an already-full switch.

A switch running at its power limit produces intermittent faults that look random.

Recorders and storage

Failed or failing hard drives, full drives with recording overwrite misconfigured, recorders that reboot, and units too old to receive firmware or support current apps.

Surveillance-rated drives exist for a reason; a desktop drive in an NVR fails early.

Network and remote access

Recorders that lost their IP address after a router change, port configurations that no longer apply, and apps that stopped working after a platform update.

Changing internet provider is a very common trigger for remote viewing failing.

Accounts and credentials

Admin passwords nobody has, accounts registered to a former employee or a previous installer, and systems where the only login left is view-only.

Recovery depends on the platform, proof of ownership and equipment condition — sometimes it is not possible.

Getting remote viewing and account control back

Losing access to your own cameras is one of the most common reasons clients call us, and one of the most fixable — up to a point. What is recoverable depends on the platform's own rules and on what evidence of ownership exists.

Usually recoverable

A forgotten password on an account you still control the email or phone number for; an app that stopped working after an update; a recorder that changed address after a router replacement.

Often recoverable

An account registered by a previous installer, where the manufacturer supports a documented ownership-transfer process and you can prove purchase or possession of the equipment.

Sometimes not

Equipment locked to an account nobody can reach, on a platform with no transfer process. A local reset may restore the recorder but lose configuration, and possibly footage.

We register the system in your name, document the credentials, remove unknown users, enable multi-factor authentication where supported and hand you the details in writing so it never happens again.

Upgrade Paths and Maintenance Plans

An ageing CCTV system rarely needs replacing all at once. There is almost always a sequence that improves the things that matter first and spreads the rest across budget cycles — provided the sequence is planned rather than improvised.

  1. 01

    Keep the cabling, change the cameras

    Existing coaxial runs can often carry higher-resolution HD analogue cameras, or be reused with adapters on selected IP systems. The cheapest meaningful improvement when the cable is sound.

  2. 02

    Change the recorder first

    A hybrid recorder can accept the existing analogue cameras and new IP cameras at the same time, so the migration can happen camera by camera instead of all at once.

  3. 03

    Add storage or extend retention

    Larger or additional surveillance-rated drives, and recording settings reviewed so the extra capacity actually turns into more days rather than more bitrate.

  4. 04

    Fix the worst cameras first

    Replace the positions that matter most — gate, entrance, till, loading bay — and leave serviceable cameras elsewhere until budget allows.

  5. 05

    Bring the network up to standard

    PoE switching, cabling and segmentation done properly, so the next camera can simply be plugged in. See structured cabling and PoE network infrastructure.

  6. 06

    Secure and document it

    Credentials replaced, users audited, firmware brought current, remote access re-established and the whole system written down — usually the step that was skipped originally.

Does CMAXX watch my cameras for me?

No. CMAXX configures the system so that you and the people you authorise can view it. We do not operate a staffed 24/7 control room and do not provide monitoring or armed response. If you need operators watching and verifying events, that is a licensed monitoring provider — and we will say so rather than imply otherwise.

Can I view my cameras from anywhere?

Where the system supports remote access and both the site and your device have working internet, yes. Some networks — certain corporate or guest Wi-Fi networks in particular — restrict the connections these apps use, in which case mobile data usually works.

How many people can have access?

Most modern systems support multiple authorised users, though the number and the available permission levels vary by platform. We set up named users at the appropriate level rather than sharing one login, so access can be granted or removed individually later.

Why am I not receiving notifications?

In our experience the phone is the cause far more often than the CCTV system. Battery optimisation, notification permissions turned off during an update, a do-not-disturb schedule or being signed out of the app all stop alerts silently. After that, check whether detection is still enabled on the camera and whether the system is online at all.

Can I get too many alerts?

Easily, and it is the most common reason people stop using their system. A camera facing a road or a busy pavement can generate dozens of notifications a day until they are muted — and a muted camera is not a security system. Motion zones, person and vehicle detection, sensitivity tuning and scheduling exist to fix this, and tuning them properly is part of the setup.

How far back can I look?

As far as your retention allows, which depends on the recorder or storage, camera count, resolution, recording mode and how busy the scenes are. It is worth testing this: pick a camera and play back a specific time three weeks ago. If it will not, you do not have the retention you think you do.

Can I download a clip to send to the police or my insurer?

On most systems, yes — export a clip from playback and share it from your device. Timestamps should be intact, which is one reason having the system time set correctly matters. We demonstrate the export process at handover, because nobody wants to learn it during an emergency.

Is it safe to view my cameras over public Wi-Fi?

The app connection is normally encrypted, so the practical risks lie elsewhere — a weak or reused password, no multi-factor authentication, or leaving the app signed in on a shared device. Use a strong unique password, enable multi-factor authentication where supported, and avoid staying signed in on a device that is not yours.

Does remote viewing use a lot of data?

Live view is the heaviest use, especially a multi-camera view at full resolution. Most systems send a lower-resolution substream to the app by default, which is why remote video looks softer than the recording — and it is usually the right trade. Notifications with a thumbnail use very little.

My installer set it up on their own account. Can that be changed?

Often, yes. Some platforms have a documented ownership-transfer process; others require a reset and re-registration, which may lose configuration. What is possible depends on the platform, the equipment and what proof of ownership exists. We will tell you which situation applies before starting, and register the system in your name once it is resolved.

Business Backup & NAS Storage Solutions

A Network Attached Storage device, commonly called a NAS, is a dedicated storage system connected to your business network. It can provide central shared folders, user access controls, fast local file access and automated backup jobs for computers or servers.

RAID is not backup

RAID can help keep a storage system available after certain drive failures, but it does not protect against every risk — including accidental deletion, ransomware, theft, fire or a badly configured synchronisation job. A strong solution separates live data from backup copies and keeps at least one protected copy away from the primary system.

Cloud & Microsoft 365 Questions Answered

The questions businesses ask before committing to a migration or a support plan.

What is Microsoft 365, and is it the same as Office 365?

Microsoft 365 is a cloud productivity platform that can include business email, calendars, file storage, Microsoft Teams, SharePoint, OneDrive and Office applications, depending on the selected licence. It also provides identity and administration tools for managing users and access. Office 365 was the earlier name used for many cloud productivity plans; Microsoft 365 is now the broader name for the service family.

Which Microsoft 365 plan does my business need?

The correct plan depends on whether users need desktop Office applications, business email, Teams, advanced security, device management, archiving or other features. CMAXX can assess your requirements and recommend a suitable approach. Features and prices must always be confirmed against current Microsoft information.

Can we keep our existing company email addresses?

In most migrations, a business can keep the same email addresses if it controls the domain. CMAXX verifies the domain, creates the target users and plans the DNS and mailbox changes so new mail is directed to the new service at the correct stage.

What systems and data can be migrated?

Possible source systems include cPanel or other IMAP mailboxes, Google Workspace, another Microsoft 365 tenant, on-premises Exchange and local file shares. A standard project may include email, calendars, contacts, user accounts and business files. Teams chats, complex SharePoint permissions, large archives, unsupported file types and third-party application data may require separate tools, licensing or specialist scope.

How long does a Microsoft 365 migration take?

The timeline depends on the number of users, data volume, source platform, internet speed, domain access, complexity and the required cutover method. CMAXX confirms the expected timeline after discovery and does not use a one-day promise for every project.

Will our email stop working during migration?

CMAXX plans the migration to reduce disruption, but a short interruption or user reconfiguration may still be required during the cutover. The exact approach is agreed in advance and validated after DNS, mailbox and device changes.

Can any provider guarantee zero data loss?

No responsible provider should present zero risk as an unconditional guarantee. A professional process reduces risk through discovery, backups, pilot testing, staged migration, validation and clear responsibilities. Important source data should remain protected until the migration is accepted.

Do we need to buy new computers?

Not necessarily. CMAXX checks operating-system, browser and application compatibility. Some users can work through web applications, while desktop applications and advanced security features depend on the selected licence and device capability. Microsoft 365 also supports a range of browsers, mobile devices and Mac or Windows computers, subject to current application support and your security policies.

What is a Microsoft 365 tenant?

A tenant is the organisation's Microsoft 365 cloud environment. It contains the company's verified domains, users, groups, mailboxes, licences, policies, SharePoint sites, Teams and administrative settings.

What is the difference between Teams, SharePoint and OneDrive?

Teams is the collaboration interface for chat, meetings and channels. SharePoint stores organisation-owned team content and document libraries. OneDrive is primarily the individual user's cloud work area. A good setup defines when staff should use each tool.

Can we control external sharing, and does CMAXX set up MFA?

Yes. Sharing settings, guest access, team membership and SharePoint permissions can be configured according to business need — external sharing should be enabled deliberately, reviewed regularly and limited to the people and information that require it. Multi-factor authentication can be included in the setup and is strongly recommended for business accounts; available methods and enforcement options depend on current Microsoft capabilities, licensing and your security policy.

What should happen when an employee leaves?

The organisation should promptly block sign-in, revoke active sessions where appropriate, preserve or transfer business data, change ownership, remove access and then reassign or remove the licence according to the retention plan. CMAXX can manage this as part of ongoing administration.

What is a NAS, and is it the same as a backup?

A NAS is a dedicated storage device connected to the business network, providing shared folders, access controls, local file storage and automated backup jobs. It is not the same as a backup: a NAS can store backups, but if it is the only copy of the data it is still a single point of failure. Important data should have separate, protected copies, including an off-site, offline or suitably secured cloud copy where required.

Is RAID a backup?

No. RAID can provide drive redundancy and help a storage system remain available after certain disk failures. It does not protect against every threat, including accidental deletion, ransomware, theft, fire, corruption or a failed synchronisation.

Should our business use cloud storage or a NAS?

Many businesses benefit from both. Cloud storage supports remote access and collaboration, while a NAS can provide fast local access, central storage and local backup. CMAXX assesses data size, internet capacity, security, recovery time and budget before recommending an architecture.

Does Microsoft 365 already back up our data?

Microsoft 365 includes service resiliency and several retention or recovery features, but those features are not the same as every business's required backup and recovery plan. Additional backup may be appropriate for faster recovery, longer retention or protection against accidental and malicious changes.

What happens if the internet is offline?

Cloud access will be limited while the internet connection is down, although some applications may retain cached or offline functionality. Local NAS access may remain available inside the office network. Businesses that rely heavily on cloud tools should consider reliable WiFi, network monitoring and an internet failover option.

Does Microsoft 365 make a business POPIA compliant?

No. Microsoft 365 can provide useful security, access and data-management controls, but POPIA compliance also depends on the organisation's lawful purpose, policies, contracts, retention decisions, staff practices, incident response and accountability.

Yes. Microsoft 365 is designed for distributed work, but the identity, device, permission and network approach must be planned — we can assess multiple sites, remote users, connectivity and support requirements as one coordinated solution.
